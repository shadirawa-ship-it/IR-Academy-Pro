1.5Intracranial Atherosclerotic Disease (ICAD)›

Anatomy & Pathology

Circle of Willis variants: Anterior communicating hypoplasia (5–15%), posterior communicating agenesis. Stenosis grading (WASID): 50–99% symptomatic disease; critical lesions ≥70% have highest recurrence risk.

Key distinction: ICAD typically affects M1 segment, P1 segment, or intracranial carotid. Risk of perforator occlusion if microcatheter placed too distally.

Procedure Overview

Approach: Femoral access via Phenom 027 guide + Marksman/Headway 27 microcatheter for angioplasty balloon advancement.

Key maneuvers:

  • Selective 3D rotation angiography to assess stenosis geometry and perforator risk
  • Soft guidewire placement (Synchro, Agility) through lesion; advance OVER wire (do not force)
  • Angioplasty balloon (2.0–3.5 mm) inflation at lesion 30–60 seconds; pressure per balloon specs
  • Stent (Enterprise, Wingspan, Neuroform Atlas) if recoil >20% or dissection

1.6Arteriovenous Malformation (AVM) Embolization›

Classification & Anatomy

Spetzler-Martin grading: Size (S: ≤3 cm, 3–6 cm, >6 cm) + Eloquence (E: non-eloquent vs eloquent) + Venous drainage (V: superficial, mixed, deep). Score 1–5; higher = higher surgical risk.

AVM angioarchitecture: Nidus (tangle of feeding arteries → draining veins without capillary bed). Multiple feeders common; must identify all to achieve complete obliteration.

Endovascular Strategy

Pre-operative embolization (goal: reduce flow, decrease hemorrhage risk during surgical resection). Curative embolization (goal: complete AVM obliteration, staged approach with multiple sessions).

Access: Femoral or radial; flow-directed microcatheterization (non-wedge placement, catheter tip in nidus) to allow maximum Onyx penetration into feeding vessels.

1.7Dural Arteriovenous Fistula (dAVF) Embolization›

Classification (Borden)

Type I: Antegrade venous drainage (low risk). Type II: Anterograde + retrograde (intermediate risk). Type III: Cortical venous reflux (high hemorrhage risk, 10% annual bleed rate).

Common locations: Superior sagittal sinus (most common), lateral sinus, cavernous sinus, tentorium.

Transarterial Approach (Preferred)

Goal: Selectively occlude feeding dural arteries (petrosquamous, middle meningeal, cavernous branches) while preserving normal dural perfusion.

Angiography: Delayed-phase imaging to see venous drainage pattern; assess for cortical venous reflux.

Materials & Stack

  • Access: 6F guide (Phenom 088 or NeuronMAX 090)
  • Microcatheter: Marksman, Phenom 027 (for coil delivery into dural feeder)
  • Embolic agents: Coils (platinum microcoils for fine dural vessels), Onyx (for more complete occlusion)
  • Technique: Carefully advance microcatheter into feeding dural artery; avoid entering cortical venous system
1.8Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis (CVT) Thrombectomy›

Anatomy

Superior sagittal sinus (SSS): Most common site of thrombosis. Lateral sinus: Second most common. Cavernous sinus: Often septic; high risk of abscess formation.

Indications for Thrombectomy

Acute ischemic stroke despite anticoagulation, worsening encephalopathy, imminent herniation, septic thrombophlebitis unresponsive to antibiotics.

Mechanical Thrombectomy Technique

Access: Femoral venous approach; advance guide catheter into internal jugular vein (IJV) under fluoroscopy.

Tools: Aspiration catheters (ACE, Sofia), stent-retrievers (Trevo, Solitaire) for organized thrombus.

Key: Gentle manipulation to avoid propagation of thrombus into cortical veins; working length must accommodate superior vena cava → IJV → sinus anatomy.

1.9Spinal Arteriovenous Fistula (Spinal AVF) Embolization›

Types & Anatomy

Dural AVF (Type I): Most common; fistula in dura, drains via medullary veins. Intradural-extramedullary (Type II–III): Rarer; intramedullary (Type IV) highest myelopathy risk.

Feeders: Segmental arteries (branches of thoracic/abdominal aorta). Critical to avoid occlusion of anterior spinal artery (ASA) — catastrophic spinal cord ischemia.

Pre-operative Imaging

High-resolution angiography of entire spinal axis (anterior/posterior circulation) to map all feeders. MRI to assess cord edema and myelopathy grade.

Selective Embolization Strategy

Access: Femoral approach with long-working-length guides (Phenom 088). Careful microcatheterization of feeding segmental artery.

Embolic: Onyx preferred for spinal nidus (flow-directed deposition). Caution: Microwire advancement into spinal cord is high-risk — use softest available wire (Glidewire, Agility) and abort if resistance felt.

Complication Risk: Spinal Cord Infarction

Anterior spinal artery syndrome (paralysis, loss of temperature sensation, preserved proprioception). No good treatment once infarction occurs — prevention is paramount.

The 5-phase endovascular journey

  1. Access & crossingNavigate small peripheral vessels and cross the lesion — nitinol wire (torque + trackability) with a TrailBlazer support catheter to drive through CTO caps without wire buckling.
  2. Vessel preparationDebulk severe calcium with HawkOne directional atherectomy — balloons cannot safely expand against rock; inflating against calcium transfers pressure to healthy wall.
  3. Advanced angioplastyChocolate PTA — nitinol cage contains radial force, blocks the shoulder effect, creates pillows & grooves. Near-100% freedom from flow-limiting dissection in registries.
  4. Drug deliveryIN.PACT Admiral DCB — urea excipient dissolves on blood contact, paclitaxel diffuses into media/adventitia through the 180-day restenotic window.
  5. Contingency (bailout)Self-expanding stent only for flow-limiting dissections (Types D–F). Goal: leave nothing behind but a healed vessel.

2.5Renal Artery Intervention (RAS)›

Anatomy & Classification

Atherosclerotic RAS: Ostial lesions (90% of cases, associated with aortic atherosclerosis); proximal/mid-vessel lesions less common.

Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD): ~10% of RAS. Medial fibroplasia (most common, "string of beads"), intimal fibroplasia, adventitial hyperplasia. Younger patients, women > men.

Clinical Indication

Flash pulmonary edema with angiographic RAS (renal artery stenosis). Refractory hypertension (blood pressure control failure on ≥3 antihypertensives) + significant RAS. Progressive azotemia (serum creatinine rise) with bilateral RAS or RAS to solitary kidney.

PTA ± Stenting Technique

Access: Femoral approach preferred; radial alternative for tortuous aorta. 6F guide (Phenom 088) positioned at renal artery ostium.

Angiography: Assess lesion length, calcification, vessel diameter distal to stenosis (target ≥5 mm for stenting).

Balloon angioplasty: 5–7 mm balloon at lesion, 30–60 sec inflation. Assess residual stenosis (<20% residual = success for FMD).

Stent placement: Indicated for ostial atherosclerotic RAS (elastic recoil/dissection), residual >30%, intimal flap. Short self-expanding stent (5–7 mm × 8–12 mm).

2.6Mesenteric Ischemia Intervention›

Anatomy & Classification

Superior mesenteric artery (SMA): Ostial atherosclerotic stenosis (TASC Type A = best for intervention).

Celiac axis: Compression syndrome (median arcuate ligament, malposition). Mimics FMD with "hook" appearance on lateral angiography.

Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI): Thromboembolism (80%), arterial dissection, low-flow states. Surgical emergency if transmural bowel necrosis develops.

Chronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI): Postprandial abdominal pain ("food fear"), weight loss. Multiple stenoses common (2–3 vessel involvement).

Acute Mesenteric Ischemia (AMI) — Endovascular Management

Approach: Femoral access, guide catheter to SMA ostium, aspiration thrombectomy (ACE 068, Penumbra) or mechanical retrieval.

Intra-arterial thrombolysis: Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) 0.1–0.25 mg bolus followed by infusion (0.01–0.1 mg/min) through endhole catheter; urokinase 250,000 U bolus + 250,000 U/hr infusion. Duration 12–48 hours with repeat angiography to assess lysis.

Adjunctive therapy: Heparin infusion (2000 U/hr) to prevent propagation. Serial lactate, base deficit to assess bowel viability.

Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia (CMI) — PTA ± Stenting

Technique: Similar to renal artery intervention. Typically requires treatment of ≥2 vessels (celiac + SMA, or SMA + IMA) for symptom relief.

Celiac axis: May require cutting balloon (debulking) for fibrous median arcuate ligament tissue.

2.7Subclavian Artery Intervention›

Anatomy & Pathology

Proximal subclavian stenosis: Risk of vertebral artery steal (retrograde flow → vertebrobasilar insufficiency). Distal subclavian: Upper extremity claudication, digital ischemia.

Etiology: Atherosclerotic (most common), Takayasu arteritis (young women, pan-arteritis), radiation-induced (prior chest/neck RT), thrombosis (thoracic outlet syndrome, effort thrombosis).

PTA ± Stenting

Access: Femoral or brachial (contralateral for proximal subclavian). 6F guide (Phenom 088) cannulates subclavian ostium.

Vertebral artery protection: Soft guidewire (Synchro, Agility) placed into distal vertebral artery BEFORE angioplasty balloon inflation (prevents vertebral occlusion if dissection occurs).

Angioplasty + stent: Similar to renal/mesenteric intervention. Stent indicated for elastic recoil, dissection, or >30% residual stenosis.

Special Consideration: Kissing Balloon (Carotid Involvement)

If stenosis extends to proximal carotid (bovine aortic arch variant), simultaneous balloon inflation in subclavian + carotid to expand both orifices and prevent carotid compromise.

Follow-up

Duplex ultrasound to assess subclavian/vertebral artery patency. Blood pressure: Compare bilateral upper extremity BP; normalization expected after successful intervention.

2.8TIPS (Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt) Creation›

Indications

Variceal hemorrhage: Refractory to endoscopic therapy + pharmacologic vasoconstriction (octreotide). Ascites: Refractory (diuretic-resistant). Budd-Chiari syndrome: Hepatic vein thrombosis with decompensation.

Contraindications: Hepatic encephalopathy (relative; risk of worsening), polycystic liver disease (insufficient liver parenchyma), uncontrolled sepsis, right atrial thrombus.

Anatomy

Preferred route: Right hepatic vein → portal vein via transhepatic tract. Alternative: Middle hepatic vein (if right vein hypoplastic). Avoid: Left hepatic vein (fissure hepatic runs through middle lobe, risk of biliary injury).

Procedure Overview

1. Access: Right internal jugular vein puncture (ultrasound-guided). Advance catheter to hepatic vein (fluoroscopic confirmation of position).

2. Portal vein needle puncture: 18G curved-tip needle (Rösch-Uchida device) from hepatic vein through liver capsule into portal vein. Confirm with portal venography (portal vein opacification).

4. Hemodynamics: Measure portal pressure gradient before/after TIPS placement. Target final gradient <12 mmHg for variceal control.

2.9Splenic Artery Embolization›

Indications

Splenic rupture (Grade III–IV): Hemodynamically stable patients; preserves splenic function (avoids OPSI). Splenic artery pseudoaneurysm: Post-trauma or post-pancreatitis. Hemophilia-related bleeding: Hyperplastic spleen with hemorrhagic diathesis.

Distal vs Proximal Embolization

Distal: Occludes segmental branches only; preserves hilum blood supply. Allows splenic salvage (OPSI prevention). Preferred for young trauma patients.

Proximal: Main splenic artery occlusion; causes total splenic infarction. Used for pseudoaneurysms (hemostasis critical) or massive hemorrhage.

Technique & Materials

Access: Celiac arteriography first; identify splenic artery origin. Advance 5F catheter into splenic artery (can be tortuous).

Embolic selection:

  • Coils: 0.035" pushable microcoils (Vortex, Tornado) for main vessel (proximal); 0.018" complex coils for segmental branches (distal)
  • Particles: 300–500 µm for parenchymal hemostasis (distal approach)
  • Glue (NBCA): For rapid hemostasis; careful deployment to avoid nonselective occlusion

Complications

  • Splenic infarction: Expected; managed with analgesia, bed rest. Fever can occur (splenic necrosis inflammatory response).
  • OPSI (Overwhelming Post-Splenectomy Infection): Risk if total splenectomy occurs; vaccinate (pneumococcal, meningococcal) pre-procedure if elective.
  • Off-target embolization: Risk to left gastric artery (fundal ulceration), left adrenal artery. Prevent with careful angiography and selective catheterization.

4.5Percutaneous Nephrostomy (PCN)›

Indications

Obstructed collecting system: Ureteral stone, malignant stricture, UPJ obstruction. Emergency drainage: Infected obstructed system (pyonephrosis) = sepsis → urgent decompression (percutaneous tube before urologic definitive treatment).

Renal function goal: Drain only if creatinine >2.5 mg/dL or significant renal insufficiency; unilateral mild obstruction without infection may not need drainage.

Anatomy & Access

Posterior calyx preferred: Ultrasound or CT-guided needle placement into posterior collecting system (lower complication rate vs anterior approach). Patient positioning: Prone or prone-oblique (decubitus).

Needle path: Posteriorly-oriented needle trajectory to avoid anterior crossing vascular structures (renal artery).

Procedure

1. Access: 18–20G needle into posterior calyx (ultrasound real-time or CT fluoroscopy). Confirm with urine aspiration.

2. Guidewire placement: Advance 0.035" guidewire into renal pelvis / ureter. Consider Amplatz superstiff (0.038") for tract dilation if large stone present.

3. Tract dilation: Serial dilators (12F → 14F → 16F → 18F) over guidewire. Stop at desired catheter size.

4. Catheter placement: Pigtail nephrostomy catheter (14–28F) with retention loop in renal pelvis. Contrast injection to confirm position + check for extravasation.

  • Infection/sepsis: Fever post-procedure; ensure drain patent. If obstruction persists + fever, consider sepsis workup (ultrasound of drain placement, blood cultures).
  • Bowel perforation: Rare but serious (colon, small bowel). Immediate recognition + surgery if identified.
  • Tube displacement: Migration into renal sinus → extraperitoneal urine leak. Replace tube over guidewire.
4.6Percutaneous Gastrostomy (PEG/PRG)›

Indications

Long-term enteral nutrition: Dysphagia from CVA (stroke), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Parkinson disease, mechanical obstruction (malignancy, stricture). Goal: Nutrition >30 days.

Contraindications: Ascites (relative; high infection risk), coagulopathy uncorrected, gastric malignancy (unless palliative).

Anatomy & Patient Selection

Gastric wall thickness: Minimum 5 mm for safe tract. Distance from skin: Assess on CT/ultrasound; excessive distance requires gastropexy (suture anchoring stomach wall to anterior abdominal wall for secure tube positioning).

Procedure

1. Positioning: Supine or left posterior oblique. CT or ultrasound to identify safe entry point on gastric anterior wall (avoid left gastric vessels if possible).

2. Gastropexy: Optional but recommended for safety. Three 18G needles with sutures anchor stomach to peritoneal wall, creating tract maturation site.

3. Gastric puncture: 18G coaxial needle into gastric lumen (within gastropexy anchors if used). Confirm with saline injection under fluoroscopy (bubble artifact in gastric lumen).

4. Tract dilation + catheter placement: Guidewire (0.035" J-tip Amplatz), serial dilators, pigtail gastrostomy catheter (18–20F initial; can upsize to 24F after 2 weeks maturation).

4.7Percutaneous Abscess Drainage›

Indications & Selection

Sepsis or fever of unknown origin: Abscess >4 cm identified on imaging → percutaneous drainage preferred (medical management alone fails in 70–80%).

Location-specific: Subphrenic, paracolic, pelvic (pouch of Douglas), psoas, peripancreatic collections.

Contraindications: Uncorrectable coagulopathy, interposed bowel (catheter can perforate GI tract), fungal abscess (controversial; some require long-term drainage).

Technique

Imaging guidance: Ultrasound preferred for real-time visualization; CT for complex/deep collections.

Needle path: Most direct route avoiding vital structures (organs, vessels). Transperitoneal approach acceptable if necessary (risk low if needle <18G).

Needle size selection: 18G Chiba needle for diagnostic tap + guidewire advancement (Seldinger technique). Coaxial 20–22G needle if catheter only needed.

Flushing protocol: Pigtail catheter 2–3 times/day with 20–50 mL normal saline (keep patent). Monitor output color/volume/odor (signs of new infection: purulent, foul-smelling).

Multiloculated abscess: May require multiple catheters (one per locule) or repositioning of single catheter. Consider imaging-guided catheter manipulation to ensure drainage from all pockets.

Fungal abscess: Candida or aspergillus (immunocompromised host); requires prolonged drainage (2–4+ weeks) + systemic antifungal (fluconazole, voriconazole). Slower response to drainage than bacterial.

  • Organ injury (delayed): Bowel perforation during drainage (rare but serious). Monitor for peritonitis; surgery if suspected.
  • Septic shock: Drain not functioning (blocked), abscess enlarges. Assess catheter patency; ultrasound to confirm position.
  • Resistant organisms: Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), anaerobes. Culture-guided antibiotics empirically started.

Indications: Hemodialysis access (high flow required), prolonged ICU support, difficult IV access.

  • Catheter fracture: Rare; can embolize fragment to heart (retrieve with interventional catheterization if symptomatic).
  • Pinch-off syndrome (PICC): Catheter compressed between clavicle + first rib. CXR shows "pinching" appearance; risk of fracture/embolization. Management: reposition or replace with new line (different site).
4.9Percutaneous Liver Biopsy›

Indications & Patient Selection

Liver disease diagnosis: Elevated transaminases of unclear etiology, suspected cirrhosis, hepatitis staging. Imaging guidance: Ultrasound real-time (preferred); CT if nodule-directed or difficult acoustic window.

Contraindications: Coagulopathy uncorrected (INR >1.5, platelets <50k), ascites (relative; collect sample fast to minimize bleed risk), right pleural effusion (lung puncture risk).

Pre-Procedure Assessment

Lab work: INR, platelet count, PT/PTT. Imaging: Ultrasound to identify optimal needle path (avoid vessels, choose hepatic segment with parenchyma away from hilum).

Procedure Technique

1. Needle insertion: Intercostal approach (needle path between ribs), patient in supine or semi-recumbent position. 18–20G core needle (Tru-Cut, automated biopsy gun) or 16G for cirrhosis (need adequate sample for staging).

2. Ultrasound guidance: Real-time visualization of needle tip approaching liver. Confirm needle in parenchyma (not vessel), then deploy biopsy mechanism (single throw of automated gun).

3. Specimen handling: Tissue placed immediately in formalin (10% formalin fixation) for histology. Fresh sample for rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) if needed.

Specimen Adequacy

Minimum length: 10 mm optimal; 6–10 mm acceptable. Portal tracts: Need ≥6 portal tracts for accurate Metavir fibrosis staging (F0–F4). <3 tracts = inadequate, consider rebiopsy.

Complication Management

  • Bleeding (rare, <1%): Minor hematoma (contained); massive bleed with hemodynamic instability requires imaging (ultrasound) + transfusion + possible angiography + embolization if arterial source identified.
  • Perforation (pneumothorax from lung puncture): Usually small, monitored CXR. If >2 cm or symptomatic → chest tube.
  • Peritonitis (bile leak): Rare; fever + peritoneal signs after biopsy. Imaging (CT) shows free fluid, possibly bile. Drain placement + antibiotics ± surgical consult.

Special Note: Cirrhotic Liver Biopsy

Coagulopathy management: INR often >1.5 in cirrhosis. Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) or prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) pre-procedure. Platelet transfusion if <50k. Biopsy still possible with expert technique; bleeding risk higher but not prohibitive.

Indeterminate lung nodule: 1–3 cm, high suspicion for malignancy on imaging (irregular margins, spiculation). Infectious workup: Fever + infiltrate (possible fungal, TB, PCP in immunocompromised). Lymphangitic carcinomatosis: Diffuse ground-glass opacities, need tissue diagnosis.

Approach: CT-fluoroscopy or CT with needle marking (biopsy at separate pass). Prone positioning preferred (posterior approach, gravity helps). Peripheral nodules easier than central (bronchial wall).

Coaxial technique: 18G coaxial outer needle placed at nodule edge; remove outer needle, advance 18–20G core biopsy needle through coaxial sheath into nodule.

Sampling: 3–5 passes typical for adequate material. Suction may improve yield (especially for cellular lesions).

3.1Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) — TACE & Staging›

Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) Staging

Stage 0 (very early): Single nodule <2 cm. Stage A (early): Single <5 cm OR 2–3 nodules <3 cm, preserved liver function. Stage B (intermediate): >3 nodules or single >5 cm. Stage C (advanced): Vascular invasion or extrahepatic spread. Stage D (end-stage): Poor performance status.

TACE (Transarterial Chemoembolization) — Conventional (cTACE)

Technique: Lipiodol (ethiodized oil, radiopaque, retention agent) mixed with chemotherapy (doxorubicin 50 mg), injected selectively into hepatic artery branches feeding tumor. Followed by embolic particles (100–300 µm) to cause ischemia.

Imaging post-TACE: Lipiodol retention on CT confirms tumor delivery. mRECIST (modified RECIST) response assessment at 4–6 weeks.

DEB-TACE (Drug-Eluting Bead TACE)

Advantage over cTACE: Calibrated microspheres (CalliSpheres, DC Bead, LC Bead M1) loaded with doxorubicin; prolonged drug elution + controlled embolization.

Bead size: 100–300 µm for hypervascular tumors (slower embolization = deeper penetration). 300–500 µm for large tumors with arterial steal.

3.2Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC) — Percutaneous Ablation›

Patient Selection & Anatomy

Ideal candidates: T1a tumors (≤4 cm), exophytic location, >1 cm from renal hilum. Caution: Central/hilar location risks collecting system injury; solitary kidney requires aggressive nephron-sparing.

Imaging: Baseline contrast-enhanced CT with 3D reconstruction to assess tumor location, size, proximity to bowel/colon.

Cryoablation (Preferred Modality)

Technique: Percutaneous placement of 17G or 20G cryoprobe (argon-based freezing, −40 °C), create 5 mm ice-ball margin beyond tumor edge (goal: necrosis extends to normal kidney).

Double-freeze technique: Initial 10 min freeze → passive thaw (3–5 min) → second 5 min freeze. Improves cell kill vs single freeze.

Imaging guidance: Ultrasound real-time for ice-ball monitoring; CT at end of procedure to confirm ablation zone encompasses tumor.

Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA)

Alternative modality; expandable electrodes (17G), power 200–300 W × 10–15 min. Faster ablation than cryo but lower complete response rate for larger tumors.

3.3Lung Cancer — Percutaneous Ablation›

Patient Selection

Indications: Peripheral NSCLC nodules 1–3 cm, medically inoperable patients or those refusing surgery. Contraindications: Central lesions adjacent to bronchi (high hemoptysis risk), lesions <1 cm from pleura (visceral injury risk).

Cryoablation Technique

Access: CT-guided percutaneous 17G coaxial needle placement directly adjacent to lung nodule (not through normal lung if possible). Place cryoprobe; freeze cycle 8–10 min (monitor ice-ball on CT).

Patient cooperation: Active breathing coordinator (ABC) or breathhold imaging to reduce motion artifact.

Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA)

Similar technique; power delivery 100–150 W for 8–12 minutes. Slower heat diffusion in lung tissue vs liver.

Complications & Management

  • Pneumothorax: 20–40% incidence (most small, asymptomatic). Observe if <2 cm; chest tube if >2 cm or symptomatic. Rarely extends during ablation after needle withdrawal.
  • Hemoptysis: Rare (<5%); usually self-limited. IPPVs (inhaled pulmonary vasoconstrictors) not standard. Hold anticoagulation post-procedure.
  • Radiation pneumonitis: If prior chest RT, risk of severe pneumonitis post-ablation. Consider alternative modalities.
  • Pleural effusion: Common; usually resolves. Tap if respiratory compromise.

Follow-up

CT 1 month post: Ground-glass opacity with consolidation expected (reactive inflammatory change). No arterial enhancement = complete ablation.

CT 3–4 months post: Zone should show progressive scarring, shrinkage. Growth = incomplete ablation or tumor recurrence → re-ablation.

3.4Bone Metastases — Ablation with Augmentation›

Anatomy & Indication

Common sites: Spine (vertebral body), femur (intertrochanteric), pelvis, proximal humerus. Pain etiology: Mechanical (fracture risk) vs inflammatory (tumor burden).

Mirel score: Predicts fracture risk (location, size, cortical destruction, pain severity). Score ≥9 = consider prophylactic fixation or augmentation.

Percutaneous Ablation + Vertebral Augmentation (Vertebroplasty)

Procedure: 17G needle to vertebral body under fluoroscopic guidance (posterolateral or transpedicular). Ablate with RFA or cryo to cytoreduct tumor. Inject PMMA (polymethylmethacrylate) cement (10–15 mL per level) for mechanical stabilization.

Cement injection: Low-viscosity (easy flow) initially; increase viscosity (slower injection) as polymerization begins. Monitor for epidural leak on fluoroscopy.

Kyphoplasty (Balloon Augmentation)

Alternative: Place balloon tamp in vertebral body; inflate to 50% height restoration, then inject cement. Creates cavity, reducing leak risk. More expensive than vertebroplasty.

Complications

  • Cement embolization: Into venous plexus (subclinical) or arterial system (cardiopulmonary). Prevent with careful low-pressure injection, fluoroscopic monitoring.
  • Epidural leak: Cord compression if large leak. Rare with vertebroplasty; more common in kyphoplasty if tamp inflation excessive.
  • Thermal injury: RFA exotherm can exceed 60 °C; risk of spinal cord injury if placed too medially. Stop if patient reports increased pain during ablation.
3.5Portal Vein Embolization (PVE) — Pre-Hepatic Resection›

Indication & Volumetry

Purpose: Occlude portal vein branches feeding tumor-bearing lobe to induce hypertrophy of future liver remnant (FLR) before major hepatic resection.

Volumetry target: FLR ≥20% of total liver volume in normal patients; ≥30–40% in cirrhotic patients (regenerative capacity lower).

Access & Technique

Transhepatic approach (preferred): Percutaneous 22G needle into peripheral portal vein branch ipsilateral to tumor. Advance guidewire, dilate tract, advance guide catheter. Selective portal vein embolization of right-sided branches (preserving left portal vein for FLR).

Alternative transjugular approach: More difficult; reserved for ascites/coagulopathy contraindications to transhepatic.
